## Risk-o-meter levels
SEBI circular SEBI/HO/IMD/DF3/CIR/P/2020/197 dated 5 October 2020, effective 1 January 2021, requires every mutual fund scheme to carry a Risk-o-meter with one of six risk levels: Low, Low to Moderate, Moderate, Moderately High, High and Very High. The level is calculated from the scheme's actual portfolio rather than its category label alone.

## How the risk level is calculated
For equity holdings the risk value considers market capitalisation (large caps are lower risk than mid and small caps), price volatility and impact cost (liquidity). For debt holdings it considers credit risk (rating of the instrument), interest rate risk (Macaulay duration) and liquidity risk. The weighted risk value of the portfolio is mapped to one of the six Risk-o-meter levels.

## Disclosure and review of the Risk-o-meter
The Risk-o-meter must be evaluated on a monthly basis and disclosed along with portfolio disclosures on the AMC website and on the AMFI website within 10 days of the end of each month. Any change in the risk level must be communicated to unit holders. The Risk-o-meter appears on the scheme's factsheet, Scheme Information Document and Key Information Memorandum, together with the statement that investors should understand that their principal will be at the indicated level of risk.

## Standard risk disclaimer
Every mutual fund communication must carry the standard SEBI-mandated disclaimer: mutual fund investments are subject to market risks, read all scheme related documents carefully. Past performance of a scheme may or may not be sustained in the future and is not a guarantee of future returns.

## Net Asset Value (NAV)
The NAV is the per-unit market value of a scheme's assets after deducting liabilities and expenses. Open-ended schemes declare the NAV on every business day. Returns are calculated from the change in NAV between two dates; Growth option NAVs include reinvested gains, while IDCW (income distribution cum capital withdrawal) options pay out part of the gains and therefore have a different NAV path.

## Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) and lump sum investment
A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) invests a fixed amount at a regular interval (usually monthly) irrespective of market levels, which averages the purchase price over time (rupee cost averaging). A lump sum investment places the entire amount at once. Each scheme specifies a minimum SIP instalment and a minimum lump sum amount, which are published in the Scheme Information Document.

## Expense ratio
The Total Expense Ratio (TER) is the annual percentage of a scheme's assets charged to cover fund management fees, distribution, registrar and other operating expenses. It is deducted from the scheme's assets daily and reflected in the NAV, so a lower expense ratio leaves more of the gross return with the investor, holding everything else equal. Direct Plans carry a lower TER than Regular Plans because they exclude distributor commission.

## Exit load and lock-in
An exit load is a charge, expressed as a percentage of the redemption amount, applied when units are redeemed before a specified holding period stated in the Scheme Information Document. A lock-in period is a statutory restriction during which units cannot be redeemed at all; ELSS schemes carry a 3-year lock-in. Many liquid, index and debt schemes have no exit load, but the exact terms differ by scheme.

## Investment horizon and asset class
Equity-oriented schemes are generally considered suitable for longer investment horizons because equity returns are volatile over short periods. Debt-oriented schemes such as liquid, short duration and corporate bond funds are generally used for shorter horizons or for capital preservation objectives. Hybrid schemes combine equity and debt to moderate volatility. Suitability depends on the investor's own goals, horizon and risk capacity; mutual fund documents advise investors to consult their financial advisers if in doubt about whether a product is suitable for them.
